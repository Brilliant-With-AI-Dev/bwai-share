How we turn a set of Mirrors into an Alignment Map

Nine steps, three points where a human decides something, and a small test of four models to see whether the cheap ones are good enough.

What this is

A design write-up of the pipeline that turns a set of 1:1 AI Mirror interviews into an Alignment Map. It describes the method only. No client material appears on this page — no names, no company, no quotes, no counts from any real engagement. Figures are illustrative and the numbers in the diagrams are made up.

Nothing here has been run yet. Every claim about what a step produces is a design claim, not a result.

The map is built from transcripts, not from the Mirror letters

This looks like a preference and isn't. Exports arrive in batches, and a letter or action plan that is missing from one export tells you nothing: the step may never have run, the export may predate the output, or the export tool may have skipped it — it is documented as doing that in some cases. You cannot tell which from the files.

So the design treats letters and plans as nice to have, never load-bearing. Anyone with a transcript is a full participant, on every question, with their own personal guide. Nothing about how someone is treated changes based on which files happened to be in the folder that day.

Otherwise you would get a different map from the same interviews depending on when someone last hit export — which is not a property any diagnostic should have.

02

How it works

Read everything and propose the questions. A human picks them. Then read each person separately against those questions, put everyone on the same scale, and write it up.

03

The one idea worth arguing about

The step that reads someone's interview is not allowed to put a number on anything. It writes down what they think and quotes them saying it. That is all.

The numbers come later, in a separate step that sees everyone at once. This sounds like a technicality and is not. A position scored in isolation is not on the same scale as anyone else's, so a set of them cannot be compared — and comparing them is the entire artifact.

04

Where you come in

Three times. Everything else runs on its own.

“You” below is whoever is running the engagement — usually the facilitator.

  1. FIRST

    You pick the questions

    You get a list of candidate questions with the evidence behind each, and three to five candidate answers to “what is this session actually for.” You cut, edit, add, and set the two ends of each question. You also supply who is facilitating and how long the session is — neither is anywhere in the interviews. This is the expensive one to get wrong, because everything after it is built on the list you approve.

  2. SECOND

    You confirm the close calls

    Some rows are obviously contested and some are obviously aligned. The ones in between are a judgement, so we flag them rather than quietly deciding. You also set how long each row deserves in the room, and how serious each landmine is.

  3. THIRD

    You release it

    Nothing leaves the machine it was built on until one question is settled with the client: whether the map can carry the real company name. Role labels do not anonymise a map for people inside the company — on most senior teams, a role is one person.

05

The model test

The question is simple: on the step that actually reads people's words, do the cheap models do as well as the expensive ones?

So that one step runs four times, once per model, holding everything else identical — same people, same questions, same instructions, same everything downstream. All four were confirmed available on the Vercel AI Gateway on the date above.

The measurement that matters

Most of it is mechanical and needs no judgement at all: does every quote actually appear in the transcript it claims to come from. A model that invents quotes is disqualified whatever else it does well.

Then the interesting one. Take one person on one question and see where all four models put them. If they all agree, that position is solid regardless of which model we pay for. If they scatter, that's a problem with the design, not the models — and no amount of money fixes it.

The rest of the scoring, and the honest caveats
  • Full sample, no shortcuts. Every person, every question, in every version. Twenty runs total.
  • Each model runs one person twice. Otherwise we can't tell “this model is worse” from “this model is inconsistent.”
  • A model grades the judgement calls — whether the stated reason actually follows from the quote. It's a different model from all four contestants, and it sees the answers unlabelled and shuffled.
  • The caveat: the grader is made by the same company as one of the four. So a fifth model re-grades a sample and we report whether they agreed. If they don't, we report that half as unreliable rather than averaging it away.
  • No hybrids. Mixing models inside one step would confound the only thing this test isolates.

The real cost is your time at the two decision points

At twelve people it is about $8 to build and $13 to test. That is noise. Which is why the design spends its effort making the two human reviews fast rather than making the model calls cheap — every close call arrives with the reasoning attached, and everything needing a ruling is collected in one place instead of scattered through the map.

07

What this design is unsure about

The big one: run the first step on the whole team

Finding the questions reads whoever has been exported so far. If a meaningful part of the team is still missing, the questions it finds may not be the ones the whole team is split on. That is the one thing that cannot be fixed later — a row can be added afterwards, but the framing every other step inherits is already set.

Everything after that first step is cheap and repeatable. That step is not. So unless the people still missing are few and peripheral, wait for the complete export before running it.

Nine smaller ones
  • Role labels do not anonymise a map internally. The second most consequential item, and it is a conversation with the client rather than anything the design can change.
  • The line between “close call” and “clear” is set by hand. A starting point to argue with, not a finding. Worth recalibrating after the first real map.
  • Whether the plain-English reasons should be written earlier. They need to be distinguishable from each other, which is easier to judge when you can see them side by side. Right now they're written after.
  • Whether spotting self-contradictions belongs in the same step as reading. It's cheaper there and uses the same material, but it means the model test also tests that.
  • Whether the placing step should be allowed to re-read transcripts. Currently forbidden, so a vivid speaker can't pull their own mark. The cost is that it can't double-check a quote it doubts.
  • We can't prove two people raised something independently — the export has no timestamps. “Separate interviews” is the strongest claim available, and the map's confidence wording slightly overstates it.
  • The cost estimates assume how long the outputs will be. Everything else is measured. If they run long, the expensive models move most.
  • The grader shares a company with one contestant. Mitigated three ways, not eliminated.
  • None of this has been run. Every claim about what a step will produce is a prediction. First real move after the questions are picked: run one person through one model and read the output before committing to the rest.
08

What it needs from the team running it

Three things, none of which are in the interviews.

  • Who is facilitating, and how long the session is. The map allocates time per question against the session length, so without it there is no plan — only a diagnosis.
  • How much of the team has actually been exported. The first step should read the whole team, for the reason in section 07.
  • Whether the map carries the real company name. Role labels do not anonymise it for an internal audience. This is a client conversation, and it gates release.
