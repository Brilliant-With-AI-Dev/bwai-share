What this sample is, and what it isn't

A take at the Alignment Map, built on an invented company so we can argue about the shape before a real team's data goes in. Everything on the Demo tab is made up. The choices behind it are the point.

The job, in one sentence

Show a leadership team where they actually agree and disagree, by how much, on the questions that bear on the thing they are about to do, so the facilitator knows where to spend the room's time. Nish's framing: the conversation is the work. The map does not catalyze change. It tells the humans which conflicts deserve their attention.

Two readers, two views, one artifact

The facilitator prepares the session. The team looks at itself in the room. The Demo tab has a toggle for each. The participant view is what the room sees: the map, and under each row the shape, each leader's reason in plain words, and the questions. No quotes. The facilitator view carries everything: each leader's reason with the quote beneath it, our one-line read of the row, a minutes estimate, and a session plan at the top. The facilitator decides what more the room sees.

The unit is a question, not a person

Every row is a strategic question the team is implicitly answering every week. Leaders appear as marks on the row, never as the subject of it. That keeps the map about the business, so a team can look at it together without anyone getting defensive. It makes maps comparable across companies, because the questions recur even when the people don't. And it makes anonymization mechanical: names become role chips and the map is unchanged. A person can still be a lens. Add ?role=ceo to the address and everyone else's marks dim.

Two tracks per row: what we say, and why

The top track is what each leader says. The bottom track is why they say it, or what they mean by it. This is the whole trick. Alignment has two failure modes that look identical from the top track alone, and they need opposite treatment in the room.

Same words, different reasons. Everyone says "stop custom builds" for five different reasons. It holds until the first hard case, then three people fold. This is the one that sells the category, because no other artifact shows it.

Different words, same view. The CEO says "move fast," the COO says "discipline," Delivery says "predictable." They mean the same thing and have been butting heads over vocabulary. Nish's addition, and the one that makes a team laugh in recognition.

The four states a row can be in

Aligned
Tight on both tracks

An asset, not a row to skip. Nish caught himself on this: spend a little time on why it's solid and what would break it.

Different words, same view
Wide on top, tight underneath

A vocabulary problem wearing a strategy problem's clothes. Agree on one word and move on. Costs ten minutes, saves a quarter.

Same words, different reasons
Tight on top, wide underneath

False alignment. Looks settled, isn't. The facilitator's job is to stress-test it before reality does.

Contested
Wide on both tracks

Real, defensible disagreement. Name it so the team stops relitigating it by accident. Gets the most room time.

How to read the shape

Two renderings of the same marks, chosen by the space. At rest, each track is a line with a dot per leader and a shaded band. The band is how far apart we are. That reads on a phone and from across a table. Open a row on a wide screen and the same marks become a 2x2: what we say runs left to right, why runs bottom to top, and the two tracks ride the edges as ticks. The four states turn into shapes: a scatter, a stack, a line, a cluster. On a phone the opened row keeps the two tracks and puts the 2x2 below them.

Room time, not a score

Each row carries a three-step indicator, Little, Some, or Most, set by how far apart the marks are and how much the question bears on the context. It is coarse on purpose. A number for the disagreement invites arguing about the number. The facilitator view adds a minutes estimate beside it, and a session plan that totals them against the session. Those are a plan, not a score.

Scoped to a context

The map names what it is for: here, Northline's Q4 planning. This is the noise filter. Plenty of disagreements are real and irrelevant. Rows are chosen and weighted by whether they bear on the thing the team is about to decide, and the session plan says what was considered and left off. The facilitator, not the map, decides what gets no time.

How the map is ordered

Matches first, then gaps, then landmines, then moonshots. Start with what we share. Spend the room's time on what we don't. Name what could blow up, sized by what it would cost. End on what could be. It is one map, read top to bottom, not four.

Questions, not conclusions, for the room

Each row ends with a question the facilitator could open with and the further questions the room should take up. Never an agenda. Implementers design the room; their identity is in that. In the facilitator view each leader's reason is the readable line, in plain words, with the quote beneath it as evidence, attributed to a role chip and never to a name. The facilitator also gets our one-line read of the row, labelled as ours. The room sees the reasons and the questions, not the quotes. The marks already say who stands where; a paraphrase beside a role chip adds no exposure a quote would not, and hiding the chip while keeping the order would be coy.

Anonymization is built in

Leaders are role chips from the first row, so a real client's map and a version we can show a prospect are the same artifact with different data. What is still open is whether role chips are enough when the questions themselves identify a company. That needs the client's sign-off, and probably a fictional wrapper like this one.

What it is not

Not the Patterns report. Patterns nest inside it. Landmines and moonshots that aren't about alignment get their own sections at the end, for the team to decide whether they deserve time.

Not a personality map. No one is scored. Marks show positions, not people.

Not an agenda. It says which conversations deserve the room's time and hands the facilitator the questions and the evidence. The order of the meeting is theirs.

Not a network diagram, and not one 2x2 for everything. Nodes and edges look sophisticated and communicate nothing at a glance. A single 2x2 flattens every question onto the same axes; each row here gets its own.

Not a one-time artifact. Not in v1, but the data model keeps versions so a team can see what closed, what opened, and what held from quarter to quarter.

Still open

  • Where do the questions come from: a fixed library we tune per context, or emergent from the conversations each time?
  • Who places the marks, and how do we show confidence in the placement?
  • What does a client need to approve for an anonymized version to be shown to a prospect?
