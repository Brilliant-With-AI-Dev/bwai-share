What this sample is, and what it isn't

A take at the Alignment Map frame, built on a made-up company so we can argue about the shape before we put a real team's data in it. Everything on the Demo tab is invented. The choices behind it are the point.

The one-sentence job

Show a leadership team where they actually agree and disagree, by how much, on the questions that matter for the thing they're about to do, so the facilitator knows where to spend the room's time. Nish's framing: the conversation is the work. The map doesn't catalyze change; it tells the humans which conflicts deserve their attention.

The unit is a question, not a person

Every row is a strategic question the team is implicitly answering every week. Leaders appear as marks on the row, never as the subject of it. Three reasons. It keeps the map about the business rather than personalities, so a team can look at it together without anyone getting defensive. It makes maps comparable across companies, because the questions recur even when the people don't. And it makes anonymization mechanical: swap names for role chips and the map is unchanged. That's why a client's version and a pitch version can be the same artifact.

Two tracks per row

The top track is what each leader says. The bottom track is why they say it, or what they mean by it. This is the whole trick. Alignment has two failure modes that look identical from the top track alone, and they need opposite treatment in the room.

Same words, different reasons. Everyone says "stop custom builds" but for five reasons. It holds until the first hard case, then three people fold. This is the one that sells the category, because no other artifact shows it.

Different words, same view. The CEO says "move fast," the COO says "discipline," Delivery says "predictable." They mean the same thing and have been butting heads over vocabulary. Nish's addition, and the one that makes a team laugh in recognition.

The four states a row can be in

Aligned
Tight on both tracks

An asset, not a row to skip. Nish caught himself on this: spend a little time on why it's solid and what would break it.

Different words, same view
Wide on top, tight underneath

A vocabulary problem wearing a strategy problem's clothes. Agree on one word and move on. Costs ten minutes, saves a quarter.

Same words, different reasons
Tight on top, wide underneath

False alignment. Looks settled, isn't. The facilitator's job is to stress-test it before reality does.

Contested
Wide on both tracks

Real, defensible disagreement. Name it so the team stops relitigating it by accident. Gets the most room time.

How the map is ordered

Matches first, then gaps, then landmines, then moonshots. Start with what we share. Spend the room's time on what we don't. Name what could blow up, sized by what it would cost. End on what could be. It is one map, read top to bottom, not four.

Open a row

The map reads at rest, on a phone, printed. Every row also opens into a bigger canvas for the person preparing the session: the two tracks at full size, why each of us lands where we do, the questions for the room, and the same five marks drawn as a 2x2.

Because over quote. In the opened row, each leader's reason is the readable line, in plain words. The quote sits beneath it as evidence. Thirty-five quotes at a glance is a wall; thirty-five reasons is a picture. Nothing is lost, and a paraphrase is harder to trace back to a person than their exact words.

The 2x2 is a second look, not the map. A 2x2 forces every question onto the same two axes. Each row here has its own. Under a row, the say track runs left to right and the why track runs bottom to top, so the four states show up as shapes: a stack, a line, a cluster, a scatter.

Degrees, not a score

The earlier draft put a decimal spread on each row. It's gone. What the facilitator needs is a room-time allocation, so each row carries a three-step "room time" indicator derived from how far apart the marks are and how much the question bears on the context. It's deliberately coarse. A number invites arguing about the number.

Scoped to a context

The map names what it's for: here, Northline's Q4 planning. This is the noise filter. Plenty of disagreements are real and irrelevant. Rows are chosen and weighted by whether they bear on the thing the team is about to decide. A different context, say an annual plan or a market entry, produces a different map from the same conversations.

Questions, not conclusions

Each opened row carries a question the facilitator could open with and the further questions the room should take up, with the evidence beside them as quotes attributed to a role chip, never to a name. The 2x2 follows as a second look. Not an agenda. Implementers design the room; their identity is in that. We give them the raw material and the trace back to why.

What it is not

Not the Patterns report. Patterns nest inside it. Landmines and moonshots that aren't about alignment get their own sections at the end, for the team to decide whether they deserve time.

Not a personality map. No one is scored. Marks show positions, not people.

Not a walkaway document. It's pre-read for the one person preparing the session, and an opening frame for the room. The team leaves with conversations they've had, not a PDF.

Not a network diagram, and not a 2x2. Nodes and edges look sophisticated and communicate nothing at a glance. A single 2x2 flattens every question onto the same axes. A row of dots on a line is readable from across a table.

Not a one-time artifact. Not in v1, but the data model keeps versions so a team can see what closed, what opened, and what held from quarter to quarter.
