How to read an Alignment Map

The conventions behind every line, mark, chip, and color on the Demo tab. This is a working discussion draft. Read this once; after that, the map should explain itself.

A working draft for discussion

Agent-authored and unreviewed. Positions and classifications are provisional interpretations of separate interviews. The facilitator details preserve evidence and qualifications.

Missing evidence stays unplaced; it never becomes an invented midpoint.

Draft scope

Five questions, two landmines, one moonshot. Prepared for discussion with Nish.

Conventions

  • Leaders are letters. Each leader is a role, never a name, shown as one letter in one color.
  • The map says "we." Every question is one the team is answering together. Nothing on it is addressed to any one person.
  • Questions, not conclusions. The map hands the room questions. What to decide, and in what order, belongs to the people in the room.
  • Nothing hides behind a hover or a tap. Everything you can learn from a slide is printed on it.
The key

How to read a line

Every question gets two lines. The top line, We say, is what each leader said the answer is. The bottom line, Because, is why they said it. The words at either end are the poles: the two ends of the answer, chosen so the spread means something. A leader's letter sits where their answer landed between them. The shaded band runs from the leftmost mark to the rightmost: the wider the band, the further apart we are.

When two or more marks would touch, they collapse into one pill with the letters in order. A pill is not a tie; it means those leaders landed within a hair of each other on that line.

Read the two lines together. Tight on top over wide underneath means we agree on the words and not the reasons. Wide on top over tight underneath means we are using different words for the same view.

And the 2x2

On a wide screen, each question slide shows the same marks as a 2x2. We say runs left to right, with the same poles as the top line. Because runs bottom to top, with the poles of the bottom line. The two lines ride the edges as a spread band and a tick per leader, so you can read either rendering off the other.

The four states turn into shapes: a scatter, a vertical stack, a horizontal line, a cluster. The small tile beside every state chip is that shape.

The four states

Each question is in one of four states, read off the two lines. Each state has a color and a shape, and both follow the question everywhere it appears. The state is a judgment made in synthesis, not a score.

Aligned
Tight on both lines

An asset, not a row to skip. Worth a few minutes on why it holds and what would break it.

Different words, same view
Wide on top, tight underneath

A vocabulary problem wearing a strategy problem's clothes. Agree on one word and move on.

Same words, different reasons
Tight on top, wide underneath

Looks settled, isn't. It holds until the first hard case. Stress-test it before reality does.

Contested
Wide on both lines

Real, defensible disagreement. Name it so the team stops relitigating it by accident.

The map is read in one order: where we match, then where we don't, then landmines, then moonshots. Start with what we share, spend the time on what we don't, name what could blow up, end on what could be.

Room time

Each question carries a three-step indicator, drawn as one, two, or three filled squares. It is set by how far apart the marks are and how much the question bears on what the team is about to decide. It is coarse on purpose. A number for the disagreement invites an argument about the number. The facilitator turns it into minutes; the room only needs the ranking.

Room time
Little. Five or ten minutes. Confirm it and move on.
Some. A real conversation, bounded.
Most. Where the session's time goes.

Landmines and moonshots: confidence and impact

Some of what comes up in the interviews is not an alignment question. A landmine is something that could go wrong that nobody owns; it wears crimson. A moonshot is an idea one person raised that nobody else mentioned; it wears indigo. Each carries two three-step indicators drawn the same way as room time.

Confidence · how sure we are
One voice raised it
Several of us raised it, independently
All of us raised it
Impact · what it would cost
Low. An owner and ten minutes.
Medium. A quarter's plan, or a good person.
High. The year, or the company.

Confidence comes from how many leaders raised the same thing without being asked. Impact is a judgment made in synthesis. Each slide says what it is, how sure we are, what it would cost, and who should own the next ten minutes. For a moonshot, cost reads as what it would take.

Views

Participants see a deck: an overview, a slide for each section, one slide per question, and one per landmine and moonshot. Every question slide shows the state, the question, the shape, each leader's reason in plain words beside their letter, and the questions for the room. No verbatim quotes.

The facilitator sees the same map as one scrolling page, with the quotes under each reason, a one-line read of each row, a minutes estimate, and a session plan. Switch with the toggle at the top of the Draft tab.

My Guide is one leader's own crib notes for the session: what they said, why, and the questions to expect where they hold one end of a line. It contains nothing about anyone else. In a real engagement it is generated at the end of that leader's Mirror interview and sent only to them.
