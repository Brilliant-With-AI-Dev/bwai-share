Looking back

Q: Let’s start with a big, easy question: as we head into the Q3 reflection, what’s most top of mind for you — or just on your mind, right now? Just click the mic and speak your truth. (And, remember: every word is private unless you decide to send it along.)

I mean, honestly? What’s top of mind is that we spent the whole quarter arguing about how we build with AI and never landed it. We shipped fine, the numbers held, but every Monday was the same conversation again and I walked into most weeks already tired of it.

Q: You said you walked in already tired of it. Before we go near that — what was your number one personal best of the last ninety days, outside of work?

The college tour with my daughter. I’d been putting it off two years. Four days, no laptop, and I didn’t open the fill-rate channel once, which for me is the whole achievement.

Q: Four days without the fill-rate channel. And the work one — what are you most proud of from the last ninety days?

One rate card for the Gulf Coast markets. Sales and ops had been stuck on it for three quarters and we got it done in about six weeks, and nobody got rolled to do it. The offices stopped hearing two different prices from us, which was embarrassing.

Q: Rate card in six weeks. On a scale of one to ten, where one is rough and ten is exceptional, how would you rate the quarter?

Seven. Maybe six and a half, honestly.

Q: Seven, maybe six and a half. What’s the single biggest reason behind that number — the biggest one, not the list?

It was two quarters in one. Delivery side, we were good — fill rate held through the summer dip, the rate card landed, engineering shipped the worker app rewrite about when they said. That’s an eight. The other side is I sat in rooms all quarter talking about the same AI decision and we walked out of every one of them without making it, and that made me dread Mondays in a way I haven’t in six years. That’s a four. So the number is both of those, and I don’t want it to bury either one. If you read a seven and think we’re fine, you’ve missed the part that costs us next quarter, which is that nobody knows who decides.

Executive

Q: Of the six EOS components — Vision, People, Data, Issues, Process, Traction — which one does the Executive team most need to improve this quarter, and what’s one example that shows why? - Is it where you’re going, or who is in which seat? - Is it the numbers you steer by, or how issues get raised and solved? - Is it whether the process is written down and followed, or whether rocks and the meeting pulse actually get done? One of these, a combination, none of them — or something else entirely.

Vision, easily. Not the poster on the wall version — I mean we genuinely do not agree on where we’re going with product and with AI over the next two or three years.

Q: You said you don’t agree on where you’re going. What’s one thing that happened that shows it?

The AI thing. Four conversations about what we build ourselves and what we buy, and every one ends with everybody nodding and nobody owning it. Then two weeks later somebody’s spun up a pilot anyway and we’re arguing about the same thing. It’s not a people problem, it’s that we never wrote it down, so everyone fills the gap with their own version.

Q: What’s the conversation the Executive team needs to have and isn’t having?

(kept private)

Q: And the single biggest issue limiting the Executive team right now?

Nobody can say who actually decides on AI. Six of us think we’re deciding it together, which means it doesn’t get decided. It’s been a month, maybe five weeks, since anybody moved on it.

GTM

Q: Of the six EOS components — Vision, People, Data, Issues, Process, Traction — which one does the GTM team most need to improve this quarter, and what’s one example that shows why? - Is it where you’re going, or who is in which seat? - Is it the numbers you steer by, or how issues get raised and solved? - Is it whether the process is written down and followed, or whether rocks and the meeting pulse actually get done? One of these, a combination, none of them — or something else entirely.

Data for this one. And not that we don’t have any — we have too much of it and I still can’t tell you what’s causing what.

Q: What’s the example that made you say that?

July. Fill rate moved and I still can’t tell you why. Marketing says the rate increase, sales says the outbound push, and both of them have a chart that says so. The data’s directionally right, I’m not saying it’s wrong, it’s that we can’t get from the chart to the cause, so we pick whichever story matches what we already wanted to do.

Q: What’s the conversation GTM needs to have and is avoiding?

Whether we still run two motions. Self-serve for single offices, a real sales motion for the groups. The honest version is the single-office side isn’t paying for itself anymore, everybody knows it, and nobody wants to be the one who says kill it because it’s somebody’s whole team. Q: And the biggest issue limiting GTM right now?

Handoffs. Marketing hands a lead to sales and it sits, sales hands an office to ops and the first shift doesn’t get filled, and every one of those is somewhere we lose an account without ever hearing about it.

Looking forward

Q: If this coming quarter were wildly successful, what would be true 90 days from now? - What would customers, or the numbers, show? - What would feel different inside the team? - What would you have stopped doing? One of these, a combination, none of them — or something else entirely.

The one I care about most is one fill-rate number and nobody walking in with a second one. That’s the whole first thing. Past that — a charter for how we build with AI, one page, boring, all six of us signed it, so when somebody spins up a pilot we point at the page instead of relitigating. Oh — and the headcount conversation. Had it, not scheduled it.
