The Leverage Map

A proposed follow-on to the Individual Mirror that helps one person decide which AI opportunities deserve attention now, which to defer or stop, and which should belong to another person or an AI system.

The proposal in plain terms

The Individual Mirror is a private guided interview that produces a Mirror Letter and Action Plan. It helps someone understand their current relationship with AI and choose four immediate next moves.

The Leverage Map would address the larger decision that remains: among all the plausible ways this person could use AI, where should they invest limited attention over the next quarter? The proposed output is a prioritised board, explicit trade-offs, and no more than five moves.

This is an internal discussion document: part proposal, part product documentation, and part evidence from an early prompt prototype. It is not a product commitment or a client-facing site.

At a glance

Who it is for. An individual who has completed the Mirror and has more worthwhile AI possibilities than time, mandate, or capacity.

What it considers. Personal goals, role accountability, manager and company priorities, current constraints, value, personal pull, and ownership.

What changes. A broad list of possible AI uses becomes a defensible allocation of attention: pursue, support, hand to AI, assign elsewhere, reduce, or stop.

How to read this walkthrough

The next section explains the product thesis and its source. The remaining sections show one simulated person's path through the proposed experience, from entry to a 90-day plan.

The interview questions, Leverage Board, trade-offs, and plan are generated output from one end-to-end prompt run. The interface is mocked; no Leverage Map product surface exists yet.

Section 1 · Product thesis

The Mirror creates personal orientation. The Leverage Map would turn that clarity into priorities.

The two experiences are related, but they do different jobs. The distinction below is the basis of the proposal.

The Mirror

A private reflection that produces immediate next actions

The Individual Mirror helps someone make sense of their current relationship with AI: what is true now, what they want, what is getting in the way, and what they should do next. It produces a private Mirror Letter and Action Plan.

Its four moves are intentionally limited. As Nish put it, “the 4 steps are not the whole map. It's just, here's the 4 things you should probably think about doing.”

Current product: Letter, Action Plan, four moves.

The Leverage Map

A decision process for allocating attention across possible AI uses

The proposed follow-on asks a different question: given what this person wants, what their role requires, what their manager and company need, and what their constraints allow, which AI opportunities should they pursue this quarter?

The result is allocative rather than reflective. It recommends what to pursue, what to support, what to hand to AI or another owner, and what to reduce or stop—and makes the consequences visible.

Proposed product: one end-to-end prompt run and this mocked interface.

Why the sequence matters

The Mirror is more than an entry screen. It creates personal context and enough trust for someone to say what they actually want, including answers that may not match the company line. Matt's point in the source discussion was that the Leverage conversation depends on that honesty: “you can't do that unless you really understand and are honest about what does winning look like to you.”

The proposal therefore begins with the person's own definition of success before adding role, manager, and company priorities. The prototype has not yet tested the actual handoff: its interview run began cold, without access to a prior Mirror.

How the opportunities become comparable

The prototype assesses every candidate on three dimensions. Value and pull determine its position on the map; ownership influences the recommended move.

Axis 1 · Value

How much would this move an outcome that matters to the person, their role, their manager, or the company?

Axis 2 · Pull

How much does the person genuinely want to do this, independent of its objective value?

Axis 3 · Ownership

Should this person retain the work and accountability, hand bounded work to AI, or assign it to another human owner? “It could be high value and you might really enjoy it, but are you really the right person?”

Ownership is the differentiating question. Work that is valuable and enjoyable can still be the wrong use of this person's attention. AI adds an option between “do it myself” and “delegate it to another person,” which changes how ownership can be designed: “‘should' used to be, I have to delegate this to a human. So people don't even really think about it for the most part… But now that you can delegate to AI, that changes the equation and it changes the questions.”

One important pattern is therefore high value, high pull, and low personal ownership: the person enjoys the work and it matters, but they should not remain its primary operator. Matt's line from the discussion was: “a lot of leaders squander an enormous amount of time in that box.” The board makes that pattern explicit instead of treating enthusiasm as proof of appropriate ownership.

Inputs that change the recommendation

Two people with the same job title may need different plans. The prototype uses four operating constraints to shape every downstream recommendation:

  • Are you evolving, consolidating, or shrinking?
  • What resources are being allocated?
  • What are employees given permission to do?
  • Who is accountable for governance and impact — a named person, or nobody?

Matt on the call: “these questions and the answers to them become the scaffold around which the leverage map coalesces.” Different answers can turn the same opportunity from “pursue” into “hand off,” “reduce,” or “stop.”

A fixed decision rule

The prototype uses consistent mappings rather than improvising a recommendation for each person: low value plus high pull means reduce it; high value plus low pull and low ownership means hand it off; high value plus high pull and low ownership is an ownership trap; high value plus high pull and high ownership means keep it and add support; low value plus low pull means stop.

The reason is on the call. Without a fixed model “it's all just too squishy… too artisanal and it doesn't scale. And also you can't know what was right and wrong” — if the only variable is the prompt wording, there is nothing to learn from a map that turned out wrong.

The model also treats the person's stated goal as an input rather than a moral test. That neutrality is part of getting an honest decision context; governance and harm constraints still apply to the resulting plan.

“Leverage should feel like, at the end of this… the emotional reaction somebody should have is like, holy fuck. If I do this and I can follow through and get it done — I win.”

Nish's description of the intended user reaction in the source discussion. It is a product aspiration, not yet validated evidence.

Open product decisions

  • The name. “Leverage Map” is the working name. “AI Leverage Mirror,” “Leverage Mirror,” and “Momentum Mirror” were also discussed.
  • Individual or team. This proposal begins with an individual. A team version would require consent, privacy rules, and a separate synthesis model.
  • Product boundary. It could be the next chapter of a saved Mirror, a separate product, or one recurring experience in a broader Mirror series.

The four-stage interview structure

  • 1 The frame
    What the person wants, what their role requires, and what their manager and company need.
  • 2 The scaffold
    Organisational direction, AI ownership, available resources, and the person's actual permission to act.
  • 3 The candidates
    The possible uses of AI, assessed on value, personal pull, and who should own the work.
  • 4 The deliverable
    A Leverage Board, explicit trade-offs, and a bounded 90-day plan.

These phase labels are internal structure, not user-facing steps. The prototype interview reached the deliverable at exchange 21.

Section 5 · Trade-offs

Every priority creates a non-priority.

This section states the consequences of the proposed plan: what Devon would stop optimising, which risks he would accept, and why some appealing work would be deferred or assigned elsewhere.

You're choosing control over the illusion of speed. Tagging waiting time will expose that a chunk of "turnaround" isn't estimating at all — it's customer ambiguity. That can make leadership uncomfortable because it removes the easy lever of "push estimating harder."

You're also choosing to force confirmation. That will slow or kill a subset of small RFQs where the only way to respond quickly is to guess. Sloan will feel that as friction; the counter-argument is that those are exactly the RFQs most likely to boomerang as rework or margin surprise.

Because there's no named AI governance owner and there's scar tissue around pricing automation ("the estimating thing"), you're intentionally avoiding AI that decides. You'll use AI to structure messy inputs, surface ambiguity, and standardise questions — with you keeping the judgment calls and accountability.

The sequencing matters: you're choosing to earn permission with measurement first. Without the waiting-time data, the gate looks like you making the scorecard worse. With the data, it looks like you making the scorecard honest — and making rework rarer.

These trade-offs were generated from Devon's interview context—the governance gap, the failed prior rollout, the scorecard, and Sloan's incentive. They are prototype output, not generic guidance added later.

Section 6 · 90-day plan

Five bounded moves turn the choices into a plan.

Each recommendation includes why it belongs, a first action, and observable evidence within 90 days. The five-move limit is a proposed product rule intended to force selection.

Do this and you'll still be fast — but now you're fast for defensible reasons, with fewer silent landmines. More importantly, you'll have the receipts to shift the conversation from "estimating is slow" to "customer ambiguity is the constraint, and here's how we manage it."

Section 7 · Status and open questions

What this prototype demonstrates—and what remains unresolved.

This artifact tests whether the product idea and generated output are coherent. It is not evidence that the workflow, interface, or business model is ready to build.

Demonstrated here
  • One self-contained interview prompt can complete an end-to-end simulated run.
  • Every question, the fifteen candidates, the scores, the trade-offs, and all five moves — generated in one unbroken run.
  • The subject. Devon and Kestrel are written in full: a world file, a profile, and a closed-world actor card that forbids inventing anything off-card.
  • The output is specific enough to reflect Devon's goals, constraints, politics, and failure risks.
Assumed or mocked
  • Every screen. No Leverage route, component, or database table exists.
  • The handoff from the Mirror. The run was cold — the interviewer had no prior Mirror to read.
  • The map itself. The model emits a table; plotting it on two axes is a product decision made here, not by the model.
  • Save, share, export, and revisit. None of it is designed yet.
Open questions
  • Scoring resolution. Six of fifteen candidates land in medium/medium. Three coarse levels do not separate them; the interview may need harder scoring or ranking within a cell.
  • Board versus plan. Four of five moves restate board rows. We need to decide whether that is useful reinforcement or redundant output.
  • Volume. Fifteen rows may be too many for a useful personal deliverable, even with the map view.
  • Ownership visibility. Ownership is the most distinctive dimension, but the two-dimensional map does not show it directly.

Internal discussion document. The immediate decision is whether this is a useful product direction and what must be learned next, not whether this particular interface should be implemented as shown.
