Screen 1 · Origin

The Mirror answers “where do I stand.” This answers “where do I point it.”

The idea has a specific origin: a 77-minute call between Nish and Matt on 7 August 2026, and the Slack thread Nish posted straight after it. Everything below is from that conversation.

The Mirror

An honest look at where you stand with AI

Nish's description of what it does, on the call: you go from “lost in the wilderness of infinite AI possibility and just copying what others are doing” to “clarity, orientation, direction, and the next 4 steps in a journey of 1,000 steps.”

He is explicit that this is the right scope, and that the four steps are deliberately not a plan — “the 4 steps are not the whole map. It's just, here's the 4 things you should probably think about doing.”

Ships today. Letter, action plan, four moves.

The Leverage Map

Where to point your firepower, and what you're giving up

The gap it fills, in his words: “where should we be pointing our firepower with AI? Like leverage. There's 1,000 things we can do.” The stance he wants the product to take is an investor's — “what are we gonna feed? What are we gonna keep? What are we gonna kill?”

So: same interview format, same voice, entirely different question. The Mirror is diagnostic. This one is allocative — it spends something, and it says what.

Does not exist. One prompt, run end to end, and these screens.

Why it needs the Mirror to come first

Not a packaging decision. The Leverage Map only works if the person is honest about what winning actually looks like for them — including the parts that don't flatter them. On the call Matt's point was that you cannot get that cold: “you can't do that unless you really understand and are honest about what does winning look like to you.” Nish finished the thought — if they've been through the Mirror first, “we've already built that comfort, trust, and credibility.”

That is the actual dependency. The Mirror is where someone learns it is safe to say the disloyal thing to this product. The Leverage Map spends that credit immediately, in its first ten minutes, asking what they personally want out of their career — not what their company would like them to want.

The three axes, and why the third one is the product

The sorting model came out of the same call, from Matt, unprompted and nearly in its final form. Every candidate gets scored three ways.

Axis 1 · Value

How much does this actually move something that matters. The obvious one. Every prioritisation framework has it.

Axis 2 · Pull

How much do you want to do it, independent of its value. Rarely asked out loud, and it decides what actually gets done.

Axis 3 · Ownership

Should you be the one doing it. “It could be high value and you might really enjoy it, but are you really the right person?”

The third axis is the one the product is really for, and the argument for why it is newly answerable is the sharpest thing in the transcript: “‘should' used to be, I have to delegate this to a human. So people don't even really think about it for the most part… But now that you can delegate to AI, that changes the equation and it changes the questions.”

Hence the diagnosis the map exists to deliver — high value, high pull, low ownership. You love it, it matters, and you are the wrong person doing it. Matt's line: “a lot of leaders squander an enormous amount of time in that box.” Nobody's quarterly planning template has a column for it.

The scaffold under the recommendations

The call also settled why two people in the same job should get different maps. Four questions constrain every recommendation downstream, and the interview will not move on until it has real answers:

  • Are you evolving, consolidating, or shrinking?
  • What resources are being allocated?
  • What are employees given permission to do?
  • Who is accountable for governance and impact — a named person, or nobody?

Matt on the call: “these questions and the answers to them become the scaffold around which the leverage map coalesces.” Answer them differently and the same list of candidates sorts into a different plan.

Deliberately opinionated

The move mapping is fixed, not negotiated per user: low value + high pull → shrink it; high value + low pull + low ownership → hand it off; high value + high pull + low ownership → the trap; high value + high pull + high ownership → keep it and build support around it; low value + low pull → kill it.

The reason is on the call. Without a fixed model “it's all just too squishy… too artisanal and it doesn't scale. And also you can't know what was right and wrong” — if the only variable is the prompt wording, there is nothing to learn from a map that turned out wrong.

It also refuses to moralise. Someone who wants AI to cut headcount gets the same quality of work as someone who wants to free their best people. Their answers are inputs, not confessions.

“Leverage should feel like, at the end of this… the emotional reaction somebody should have is like, holy fuck. If I do this and I can follow through and get it done — I win.”

Nish, on the bar. That sentence is the only acceptance criterion the prototype has, and it is quoted verbatim at the top of the interview prompt as the one question the build is meant to answer.

What is still unsettled

  • The name. “AI Leverage Mirror,” “leverage mirror,” “momentum mirror” were all live on the call. Nish's expectation: the “AI” drops off eventually and it is just a Leverage Mirror.
  • Individual or team. Both were wanted; individual was chosen to go first because the group version's complexity scales far faster. The group synthesis is a separate, later thing.
  • Whether it's one product or a series. The other idea floated was a new Mirror every month, all the same shape — this being the second one.

Screen 5 · The cost

What you're choosing against.

The section that makes it a decision instead of a list. Opinionated by design, and specific to what Devon said.

You're choosing control over the illusion of speed. Tagging waiting time will expose that a chunk of "turnaround" isn't estimating at all — it's customer ambiguity. That can make leadership uncomfortable because it removes the easy lever of "push estimating harder."

You're also choosing to force confirmation. That will slow or kill a subset of small RFQs where the only way to respond quickly is to guess. Sloan will feel that as friction; the counter-argument is that those are exactly the RFQs most likely to boomerang as rework or margin surprise.

Because there's no named AI governance owner and there's scar tissue around pricing automation ("the estimating thing"), you're intentionally avoiding AI that decides. You'll use AI to structure messy inputs, surface ambiguity, and standardise questions — with you keeping the judgment calls and accountability.

The sequencing matters: you're choosing to earn permission with measurement first. Without the waiting-time data, the gate looks like you making the scorecard worse. With the data, it looks like you making the scorecard honest — and making rework rarer.

Every claim here traces to something Devon said in the interview — the governance gap, the scar, the scorecard, Sloan's incentive. None of it is generic.

Screen 6 · The plan

Five moves. Never more than five.

Each one has a first step small enough to start on Monday and one observable signal inside 90 days.

Do this and you'll still be fast — but now you're fast for defensible reasons, with fewer silent landmines. More importantly, you'll have the receipts to shift the conversation from "estimating is slow" to "customer ambiguity is the constraint, and here's how we manage it."

Screen 7 · For review

What's real here, and what isn't.

So the walkthrough isn't mistaken for a working product.

Real
  • The interview prompt. Live in the repo, self-sufficient, already driving runs.
  • Every question, the fifteen candidates, the scores, the trade-offs, and all five moves — generated in one unbroken run.
  • The subject. Devon and Kestrel are written in full: a world file, a profile, and a closed-world actor card that forbids inventing anything off-card.
  • The design language. Cool-slate tokens, Newsreader and Outfit, one accent pop.
Mocked
  • Every screen. No Leverage route, component, or database table exists.
  • The handoff from the Mirror. The run was cold — the interviewer had no prior Mirror to read.
  • The map itself. The model emits a table; plotting it on two axes is a product decision made here, not by the model.
  • Save, share, export, and revisit. None of it is designed yet.
What it raises
  • The middle is crowded. Six of fifteen candidates land in medium/medium. Three coarse levels can't separate them — either the interview scores harder, or the map ranks within a cell.
  • The plan repeats the map. Four of five moves are board rows restated. Worth deciding whether that's reinforcement or redundancy.
  • Fifteen rows is a lot to hand someone. The map exists because the table was hard to read.
  • Ownership is invisible. "Should you" is the third axis and the most original question in the product. It's currently buried in a table column.

Built to be judged and thrown away. If the shape is right, the next step is building it in the app with real components; if it isn't, nothing was spent finding out.
