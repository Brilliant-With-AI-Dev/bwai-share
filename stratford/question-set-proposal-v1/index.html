Working session: shaping the Mirror for the 17th.

Every participant will have a private Mirror conversation before the offsite. Their answers become one readout for you. This session is for deciding, with you, what that readout should be — then walking through the questions participants will be asked, and a set we'd propose adding.

1What you receive before the offsite

Sixteen private conversations produce a lot of material. The readout is what turns it into something you can run a room from. Here are three ways it could be organised. They are different in kind, so which one you'd reach for tells us how you intend to run the day.

Three shapes

A · The assumptions map

Organised against your sixteen strategy assumptions. For each one: where the group actually lands, how contested it is, and what the people who disagree say would have to change. Opens the room at "which of these do we need to re-settle today."

B · The tension board

Organised by the fault lines that cut across everyone's answers — unit economics against firm economics, integration against autonomy, ambition against capacity. For each: the two positions, roughly who holds each, and what each side says would move them. Built for running a debate.

C · The decision queue

Organised by the decisions the day has to close. For each: what is at stake, where people already agree, where they don't, and the specific thing the room must settle. Built for driving to outcomes.

What we'd like to work through with you

  1. Walk us through how the 17th is structured. What are the blocks, and which of them have to end in a decision rather than a discussion?
  2. Which decision are you most worried you won't close?
  3. Is the readout yours alone, or does it go to all sixteen? Does that change what should be in it?
  4. In the room, what would you do with it — project it, quote from it, or keep it beside you?
  5. There are three different things we could show you: where people agree, where they clearly split, and where they think they agree but don't. Which of those is most valuable to you on the day?
  6. When people disagree, do you want to see who holds which position, or just how the weight falls?
  7. If the readout showed you that one of your sixteen assumptions is genuinely contested, what would you do with that on the day?
  8. Of the three shapes above, which would you actually use on the 17th — and what is missing from all three?
  9. If you could have only one page on the wall for the whole day, what is on it?
  10. What would make you close this after five minutes and not open it again?
  11. What did the pre-work for your last planning session fail to give you?

3Questions we would add

Twenty-one candidates. Each one asks for a position or a trade rather than a description — where you stand, what you'd give up, what you'd name — because the offsite's purpose is decisions, and reflective answers alone don't shorten the room's path to one. Each states its own premise, so nobody needs a document open to answer it. This is a bank to choose from, not a sequence.

Delivering as one firm

  1. The plan takes the position that when a client needs more than one part of Stratford, that should happen by design rather than by goodwill. Think of a recent opportunity where that would have changed what actually happened. What would have had to be different?Turns the integration argument into one incident with a name and a date.
  2. Today, when two parts of the business want the same person at the same time, who actually decides? And who should?Decision rights, concrete enough to answer in a sentence.
  3. If you could write one rule about how people are shared across the business, and it took effect tomorrow, what would it say — and who would find it hardest to live with?The second half surfaces the real opposition, named.
  4. The plan says the way we're organised today gets in the way of working as one firm. Where have you run into that yourself — and was it really the structure, or something else wearing the structure's clothes?Tests whether structure is the cause or the alibi. Either answer is worth having.

The economics

  1. Our current plan sets a stretch gross-profit target just under 33%. There is a view in the leadership group that 40% gross margin and 20% before-tax profit are achievable in every business line. Where do you land — and what would have to change in pricing, staffing or delivery for the higher number to be real in your area?The gap between those two numbers is the most consequential open question in the plan.
  2. If we finish next year in the low thirties again rather than at forty, what will the reason have been? Name the most likely one.A diagnosis rather than a hope. Sorts people into pricing, mix, delivery, or belief.
  3. What would you have to stop doing to run your part of the business at forty points? Name the thing you would actually give up.A trade, not an aspiration.
  4. The plan says clients increasingly pay for measurable outcomes rather than traditional deliverables. Which of your current engagements could not be priced that way today, and what specifically stops it?Tests the value-pricing thesis against live work instead of in principle.

Credit and reward

  1. What does a person here have to do to be seen as having had a great year? Is that the same as what the strategy is now asking for?The reward system as people actually experience it, rather than as designed.
  2. Describe the last time you put your people or your time into work you didn't own. What did you get for it — and was it enough?An honest answer about credit, without asking anyone to theorise about credit.
  3. If how we're rewarded changed so that firm results counted for more than your own unit's, what would you do differently on the Monday after?Makes the compensation question operational rather than philosophical.

Business development

  1. The plan says business development has to become something the firm does, rather than something particular individuals happen to be good at. What has to be true for that to work — and what would you personally have to take on, or give up?Most people support this in principle. The second half is where it costs something.

Where we play, and what we're good at

  1. We've said we'll build and demonstrate deep expertise in selected industries. Name the industry you'd choose, and one client we'd have to win to prove we have it. If you wouldn't choose yet, say what you'd need to know first.This assumption is marked as amended rather than settled. "I wouldn't choose yet" is a legitimate and useful answer.
  2. Do we have the people to run the firm we're describing? If not, name the first gap you'd close — and whether you'd close it by developing someone, hiring, or working differently with associates and partners.Rarely asked out loud about the leadership group itself.
  3. We've said technology and AI are reshaping both our clients and our own business. In your work, where would AI change the answer to "how many people do we need on this" — and where do you think it won't work yet?Makes AI a resourcing question, which is where it shows up in the numbers.

What's settled and what isn't

  1. Name one thing you believe has already been decided that you suspect other people still think is open.Surfaces the misalignment in one question, with no grid required.

The day itself

  1. What are we most likely to avoid deciding on the 17th — and what will we call it instead?Names the polite-failure mode while there is still time to prevent it.
  2. Name the one decision that, if we leave without it, makes the rest of the decisions not matter.Forces a ranking without asking anyone to rank.
  3. Where the room splits, the CEO has said he'll make the call. What would you need to see or hear to back a decision that went against you, rather than just live with it?Separates alignment from compliance, and prepares people to lose well.

What happens after

  1. What will be on your plate on 1 October that isn't there today — and what came off to make room?Cannot be answered without doing the arithmetic.
  2. It's three months after the offsite and not much has changed. What happened? Be specific.A pre-mortem. People describe the failure accurately when asked to predict it rather than prevent it.
