Stratford · Offsite Strategy Mirror

Working session: shaping the Mirror for the 17th.

Every participant will have a private Mirror conversation before the offsite. Their answers become one readout for you. This session is for deciding, with you, what that readout should be — then going through the conversation itself, section by section, with the questions we'd consider adding.

  1. What you receive before the offsite. Three possible shapes for the readout, and the questions we'd like to work through with you to choose.
  2. How the conversation works. The pattern each section follows.
  3. The questions, section by section. The ten as they stand, with example follow-ups, and under each section the two we'd most consider adding.

1What you receive before the offsite

Sixteen private conversations produce a lot of material. The readout is what turns it into something you can run a room from. Here are three ways it could be organised. They are different in kind, so which one you'd reach for tells us how you intend to run the day.

Three shapes

A · The assumptions map

Organised against your sixteen strategy assumptions. For each one: where the group actually lands, how contested it is, and what the people who disagree say would have to change. Opens the room at "which of these do we need to re-settle today."

B · The tension board

Organised by the fault lines that cut across everyone's answers — unit economics against firm economics, integration against autonomy, ambition against capacity. For each: the two positions, roughly who holds each, and what each side says would move them. Built for running a debate.

C · The decision queue

Organised by the decisions the day has to close. For each: what is at stake, where people already agree, where they don't, and the specific thing the room must settle. Built for driving to outcomes.

What we'd like to work through with you

  1. Walk us through how the 17th is structured. What are the blocks, and which of them have to end in a decision rather than a discussion?
  2. Which decision are you most worried you won't close?
  3. Is the readout yours alone, or does it go to all sixteen? Does that change what should be in it?
  4. In the room, what would you do with it — project it, quote from it, or keep it beside you?
  5. There are three different things we could show you: where people agree, where they clearly split, and where they think they agree but don't. Which of those is most valuable to you on the day?
  6. When people disagree, do you want to see who holds which position, or just how the weight falls?
  7. If the readout showed you that one of your sixteen assumptions is genuinely contested, what would you do with that on the day?
  8. Of the three shapes above, which would you actually use on the 17th — and what is missing from all three?
  9. If you could have only one page on the wall for the whole day, what is on it?
  10. What would make you close this after five minutes and not open it again?
  11. What did the pre-work for your last planning session fail to give you?

2How the conversation works

The conversation is spoken, private, and organised into sections. Each section follows the same pattern.

  1. The set questions come first. These are the ones on the following pages. The Mirror can adjust the phrasing to the person and to what they've already said, but it asks every one.
  2. Then one follow-up of its own. Based on everything it has heard so far, it asks the single question that would most fill in what's still missing on that topic. The follow-ups shown under each question below are examples of the directions it might take — starting points, not a script.
  3. Then an open door. "Before we move on, is there anything you wish we'd covered?" Most people say no. When something surfaces here, it tends to matter.

Participants review everything they've said before any of it is shared. What reaches the readout is what they've approved; the conversation itself stays private.

The offsite itself

working name

Question 1 · What should the offsite achieve?

Imagine you're heading home after the offsite. What would need to have happened for you to think, "That was the best offsite I could have hoped for"?

Example starting points
  • You feel clearer — about the strategy, the priorities, and your part in what comes next.
  • A difficult decision has been made — or, at least, discussed honestly and openly.
  • Something that needed saying is finally out in the open — and it feels easier to move forward.

Purpose: Surface personal definitions of success that formal objectives might miss.

Question 2 · What must not remain unresolved?

Imagine someone asks afterward, "Did you finally talk about it?" What might "it" be?

Example starting points
  • Is it something people openly disagree about — or something they keep discussing without reaching the real issue?
  • Would progress mean finding an answer — or first recognizing that people see the problem differently?
  • Is the difficult part saying it clearly, hearing someone's response, or deciding what follows?

Purpose: Find avoided issues and the conditions for a candid, productive discussion.

Questions we could add here

Add 1

What are we most likely to avoid deciding on the 17th — and what will we call it instead?

Names the polite-failure mode while there is still time to prevent it.

Add 2

Name one thing you believe has already been decided that you suspect other people still think is open.

Surfaces the gap between what's settled and what people think is settled, in one question.

Client value and economics

Question 3 · What makes Stratford valuable to clients?

Bring to mind a client moment that made you think, "That's why they work with Stratford." What was happening?

Example starting points
  • Did the client see something differently, make a better decision, or accomplish something they couldn't otherwise?
  • Was the value mostly in someone's expertise — or in how people and capabilities came together?
  • Was that something Stratford can deliver repeatedly — or something exceptional people made happen?

Purpose: Identify concrete sources of client value and what would make them repeatable.

Question 4 · How do we reach the proposed margins?

Bring to mind a recent project where the client was really pleased. How did that feel when you looked at what Stratford put in and got back?

Example starting points
  • Did the client's satisfaction and the economics tell the same story — or did one feel better than the other?
  • Was the difference mostly in the price, the effort involved, or what the client valued?
  • Against the proposed 40% gross margin and 20% before-tax profit, does that example make you hopeful, doubtful, or curious?

Purpose: Surface the evidence and assumptions behind reactions to the proposed financial ambition.

Questions we could add here

Add 3

What would you have to stop doing to run your part of the business at forty points of gross margin? Name the thing you would actually give up.

A trade, not an aspiration. It can't be answered without committing to something.

Add 4

The plan says clients increasingly pay for measurable outcomes rather than traditional deliverables. Which of your current engagements could not be priced that way today, and what specifically stops it?

Tests the value-pricing idea against live work instead of in principle.

Working as one firm

working name

Question 5 · What does "one Stratford" mean to a client?

Picture a client who needs help from more than one part of Stratford. Walk me through where it feels like one firm — and where they might notice the joins.

Example starting points
  • Would they notice repeated explanations, separate recommendations, or uncertainty about who sees the whole picture?
  • Would improving that require more consistency — or more freedom to work across boundaries?
  • What would you want to preserve because a particular practice does it especially well?

Purpose: Reveal client-visible friction, useful differences, and specific implications of integration.

Question 6 · Who takes this opportunity forward?

Think of an opportunity that involved another practice — or could have. Where did you find yourself thinking, "Who takes this forward"?

Example starting points
  • Was it unclear who opens the door, who shapes the offer, or who commits people?
  • Would a clearer agreement help — or did people understand their roles but face competing commitments?
  • If it succeeded, would contributions be recognized fairly? If it struggled, would responsibility be equally clear?

Purpose: Clarify business-development roles, staffing authority, recognition, and ownership.

Questions we could add here

Add 5

Today, when two parts of the business want the same person at the same time, who actually decides? And who should?

Decision rights, concrete enough to answer in a sentence.

Add 6

The plan says the way we're organised today gets in the way of working as one firm. Where have you run into that yourself — and was it really the structure, or something else wearing the structure's clothes?

Tests whether structure is the cause or the alibi. Either answer is worth having.

Capabilities and AI

working name

Question 7 · Where could AI change the business?

Pick a piece of client work you know well. Suppose the routine parts suddenly took much less effort. What would you want to do with the room that opened up?

Example starting points
  • Deliver the same result faster — or give the client something more valuable?
  • Spend more time exercising judgment — or reshape the service so different work becomes possible?
  • Would the first change be in client delivery — or in the way the business supports it? What makes you curious or hesitant?

Purpose: Make AI-first ambitions tangible through service possibilities and practical conditions for trying them.

Question 8 · Which capabilities should we deepen?

Think of a client opportunity you'd love Stratford to win. Imagine saying, without stretching, "Stratford is exactly the firm for this." What would make that true?

Example starting points
  • Deeper industry knowledge, a distinctive approach to the problem, or a stronger combination of capabilities?
  • Is Stratford close already — or is something important missing?
  • Would that capability come from developing people, hiring, or working differently with associates and partners?

Purpose: Connect market choices and differentiation to capability investment and available talent.

Questions we could add here

Add 7

We've said we'll build and demonstrate deep expertise in selected industries. Name the industry you'd choose, and one client we'd have to win to prove we have it. If you wouldn't choose yet, say what you'd need to know first.

This assumption is marked as amended rather than settled, so declining to choose is a legitimate and useful answer.

Add 8

We've said technology and AI are reshaping both our clients and our own business. In your work, where would AI change the answer to "how many people do we need on this" — and where do you think it won't work yet?

Makes AI a resourcing question, which is where it shows up in the numbers.

Incentives and rewards

working name

Question 9 · What do current incentives encourage?

Imagine helping another part of Stratford win important work, knowing it might make your own numbers harder to hit. What would go through your mind?

Example starting points
  • Would you expect the contribution to count — or feel you were relying on goodwill?
  • Would the biggest concern be recognition, compensation, capacity, or explaining the result afterward?
  • Would a different measure, a different reward, or a clearer agreement help most?

Purpose: Expose tensions between firm-wide outcomes and the choices current incentives encourage.

Questions we could add here

Add 9

What does a person here have to do to be seen as having had a great year? Is that the same as what the strategy is now asking for?

The reward system as people actually experience it, rather than as designed.

Add 10

If how we're rewarded changed so that firm results counted for more than your own unit's, what would you do differently on the Monday after?

Makes the compensation question operational rather than philosophical.

Making change happen

Question 10 · What would show that change is happening?

Imagine it's a month after the offsite. You notice something and think, "Okay — this is really happening." What did you notice?

Example starting points
  • Someone made a difficult choice — or something changed in everyday work?
  • A commitment gained an owner and actual capacity — or something else came off the list?
  • If progress slipped, would someone notice early and make a correction? What would help them see it?

Purpose: Identify credible signs of execution, resource needs, useful measures, and accountability.

Questions we could add here

Add 11

What will be on your plate on 1 October that isn't there today — and what came off to make room?

It can't be answered without doing the arithmetic.

Add 12

It's three months after the offsite and not much has changed. What happened? Be specific.

A pre-mortem. People describe the failure accurately when asked to predict it rather than prevent it.
